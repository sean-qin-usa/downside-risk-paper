# What decides lambda, how much the closed form accounts for, and the earnings calendar (results of 2026-11-19, second pass)

This note continues `docs/SHOCK_RESPONSE_RESULTS.md`. That note measured lambda, the share of a large shock's GARCH
variance impact that is real, and left four questions open: what decides lambda, whether the closed form predicts the
size of the flexible models' gain and not only where it sits, why GARCH-t is also beaten on quiet days, and what the
learned tail adds. Scripts: `tools/r76_origin_accounting.py` (blocks H, K, Q, C, S) and `tools/r78_calendar_garch.py`.
Predictions were written in the script headers before each run and are graded in Section 3. Result files:
`results/paper/diagnostics/r76/` (seven universes) and `r78/` (the two US stock universes). The tables in Section 5 are
generated by `tools/make_r76_summary.py`.

Level is 2.5% unless stated. "Edge" is the percentage reduction in pinball loss against the named reference; DM is the
Diebold-Mariano statistic on per-date loss differences (Newey-West, 10 lags). The "announcement window" is an earnings
announcement date and the next trading day; "other days" are all the rest.

## 1. Findings

**GARCH-t's VaR is breached on one announcement day in seven.** On the two US stock universes the announcement window
is 3% of trading days. The mean squared GARCH-t residual is 6.1 on announcement dates and 8.5 on the day after on the
canonical panel (7.1 and 4.7 on US large caps), against 0.99 and 0.91 on other days. The 2.5% VaR is breached on
14.4% and 13.3% of window days and the 1% VaR on 10.0% and 9.6% (Table 4). So 3% of days hold about 15% of all 2.5%
breaches and about a quarter of all 1% breaches. No model without the calendar does better there: Beta-t-EGARCH is
breached on 13.9% and 13.8% of window days, the pooled trees on 14.9% and 13.6%.

**The two errors are one fact.** The announcement return is a scheduled one-off. GARCH-t does not widen for it
beforehand, and then carries it in the variance for weeks afterwards (lambda near 0.1, the earlier note). The flexible
models learn the second error and not the first: on window days the pooled trees and the neural network are slightly
worse than GARCH-t (edges of -3.3% and -0.3% on the canonical panel, -0.7% and -1.1% on US large caps).

**The calendar is worth two to four times the gap between GARCH-t and the best flexible model.** Multiplying the
scale on the two window days (multipliers chosen on pre-test rows) and discarding earnings shocks afterwards gains
6.6% over GARCH-t over all days on the canonical panel (DM 8.2) and 4.8% on US large caps (DM 13.3); at the 1% level
12.2% and 8.5%. Beta-t-EGARCH and the flexible models gain 0.9% to 2.4% over GARCH-t on the same rows (0.6% to 2.8% at the 1% level). Nearly all of the calendar's gain
comes from widening on the window days (5.4% and 3.9%); discarding the earnings shock afterwards adds 0.6% on both
universes, which is small over all days because it acts on few rows.

**Fitted with announcement days masked, GARCH-t has thinner tails.** When window days add nothing to the likelihood
and their returns do not enter the variance recursion, the median degrees of freedom rise from 3.9 to 5.0 on the
canonical panel and from 5.3 to 6.8 on US large caps (Table 5). Part of the fat tail that GARCH-t fits to stock
returns is the earnings calendar. The control, fitted by the same code with nothing masked, reproduces the stored
GARCH-t (edge 0.0%).

**Over all days the masked calendar GARCH-t beats every flexible model** by 3.1% to 6.0% at the 2.5% level and 6.7%
to 12.8% at the 1% level, with DM between 4.8 and 10.7 (Table 6). That comparison is unequal, since the flexible
models were not given the calendar.

**Like for like, the flexible models keep a small edge at 2.5% and none at 1%.** Every model was given calendar
multipliers chosen on the first half of the test dates and compared on the second half (Table 7). The calendar adds
7% to 8% to every model on the canonical panel and about 5% on US large caps (13% to 15% and 9% to 11% at the 1%
level). With the calendar in all of them:

- Beta-t-EGARCH and the flexible models still beat GARCH-t by 1.1% to 3.8% (DM 4 to 8);
- the masked GARCH-t recovers 2.5% of that on the canonical panel and 1.1% on US large caps;
- the best flexible model (trees on the robust scale) beats the masked GARCH-t by 1.3% on the canonical panel (DM 3.0)
  and 0.6% on US large caps (DM 2.1) at the 2.5% level;
- at the 1% level no flexible model beats the masked GARCH-t by a significant margin on the canonical panel (edges
  between -0.7% and +0.3%), and on US large caps only the trees on the robust scale do (0.7%, DM 2.2).

**On other days the flexible models keep their lead over the masked model.** Masking gains 1.1% over GARCH-t on other
days on both universes, less than predicted, and the trees on the robust scale still beat the masked model there by
1.7% and 1.4%. The masked model is also breached too often on other days (2.85% and 2.99%). So the calendar is not the
whole of what the flexible models have learned.

**The closed form predicts the size of the gain, not only its place.** With lambda fixed at its pre-test value the
closed form gives, row by row and without any test return, the expected loss difference between GARCH-t and the
correct quantile (Table 3). Over all days it predicts 1.4%, 1.2%, 0.8% and 3.0% on the canonical panel, US large caps,
international large caps and crypto, and 0.1% to 0.3% on currencies and the two ETF panels. The pooled trees gained
1.6%, 1.4%, 1.5% and 3.6%, and nothing significant on the ETF panels. Across the ten deciles of D the predicted and
realized edges have a correlation above 0.98 on every stock panel. Across names the link is weak (Spearman 0.2 to
0.3 for the trees).

**Within equities, lambda follows the origin of the shock.** A shock is firm-specific when the market move that day
accounts for under a tenth of it, market-wide when it accounts for half or more (Table 1). Firm-specific shocks are
74% to 87% of the large shocks on the three stock panels and have lambda of 0.10, 0.10 and 0.15. Market-wide shocks
are 1% to 6% of them on stocks and 70% on equity ETFs. Their lambda in the top decile is 0.45 on US large caps, 0.80
on international large caps and 1.10 on equity ETFs; among all rows with D above 0.10 it is at the upper end of the
grid (1.5) on all three, and GARCH-t is breached too often after them (3.0% to 3.6%). After a market-wide shock
variance stays higher than GARCH says; after a firm-specific one it does not stay high at all. On US large caps the
shocks that are neither earnings nor market-related have lambda 0.15, close to the 0.10 of earnings shocks: what matters is
that the shock is the firm's own, not that it is an earnings release.

**Outside equities the origin does not order lambda.** In crypto lambda is near zero in every class in the top
decile. In currencies the "market-wide" class is dominated by one date (the 2016 referendum) and has lambda near
zero. In bond and commodity ETFs every class has lambda near 1 or above, and the panel mean is not a meaningful market.

**The learned tail does two things.** In the first five days after a large shock it widens Beta-t-EGARCH's VaR (by
6% on US large caps and 18% on international large caps), where Beta-t-EGARCH is breached 4.2% and 4.6% of the time:
the robust filter discards a shock whose real effect lasts about a week, and the learned tail puts that week back. Its
edge there is 4% to 11% but those rows carry a tenth or less of its total gain. Everywhere else it narrows the VaR
slightly (2% on US and international large caps, 6% on the canonical panel) for an edge of about 1%, and 60% to 80% of
its total gain comes from the eight lowest score deciles (Table 8). On window days it is worse than Beta-t-EGARCH. A
regression of what it does to the 2.5% quantile on D, shock age, the score decile and the level of GARCH volatility
explains 4% to 19% of the variance, so most of what the learned tail does is not described by these shock variables.

## 2. What this changes

The earlier note's answer was: flexible models beat GARCH on stocks because they discard one-off shocks, and most of
those shocks are earnings announcements. This pass adds four things.

1. The same scheduled event produces a larger error in the other direction, on the announcement days themselves,
   which no model in the comparison addresses and which a calendar removes.
2. The comparison of flexible models with GARCH on single stocks is in large part a comparison of how each copes
   without the calendar. With the calendar in every model the gap is 1% to 4% over GARCH-t and at most about 1% over a
   GARCH-t fitted with announcement days masked.
3. The closed form accounts for the size of the gain panel by panel.
4. Lambda is a property of the kind of shock (firm-specific or market-wide) within equities, which is why it is low
   on stocks and near 1 on equity ETFs.

Limits. Earnings dates come from one free source, for 151 of the 200 canonical names (147 fitted) and all 201 US
large caps (199 fitted); the split of the reaction between the announcement date and the day after differs between
the two universes, which points to timing conventions in the source. The model uses the realized announcement date
and so assumes the date was known one day ahead. The flexible models were given the calendar only through two
multipliers on their VaR, not as a feature at training. That earnings days are more volatile is long established;
whether published VaR comparisons of machine-learning models and GARCH on single stocks control for it has not been
checked here and is an open item of the worklist.

## 4. Open after this pass

- The flexible models with the calendar as a training feature, not as two multipliers after the fact.
- What the flexible models' remaining 1% to 2% on other days is. The learned tail's adjustment is mostly not a function
  of the shock variables examined here.
- A second source for announcement dates and their timing (before the open or after the close).
- Other scheduled one-offs: central-bank and macro release days for the ETF, currency and bond panels.
- The literature on announcement effects in volatility models and whether VaR comparisons control for the calendar.

### 6.6 Prior work found in a search pass

A search of the literature was run on the same day. Read levels are stated because several publishers refused
automated access; nothing below has been checked beyond that level, and no citation search was run.

Established before us:

- Earnings days carry extra, anticipated variance: Patell and Wolfson (1979, 1981), Donders and Vorst (1996),
  Dubinsky, Johannes, Kaeck and Seeger (2019, option pricing with jumps at earnings dates; full text read).
- Announcement multipliers in GARCH with separate persistence for announcement shocks, for macro news: Jones, Lamont
  and Lumsdaine (1998; abstract), Li and Engle (1998, "filtered GARCH", working paper; full text). For single stocks:
  an earnings dummy in a robustness section of Maheu and McCurdy (2004; working-paper version read), and a
  dissertation chapter (Deng 2008; not read).
- Jump or news shocks feed less into later variance than ordinary shocks: Maheu and McCurdy (2004), Andersen,
  Bollerslev and Diebold (2007; indices, currencies, bonds), the outlier-correction papers (Franses and Ghijsels
  1999, Charles 2008). Corsi, Pirino and Reno (2010) find the opposite for jumps measured differently and must be
  addressed.
- Earnings as a predictor in a machine-learning volatility comparison on single stocks: Christensen, Siggaard and
  Veliyev (2023; full text), where it is one indicator among nine extra predictors.
- Idiosyncratic against systematic jumps and later volatility is named as open by Patton and Sheppard (2015; full
  text); two working papers close to it were not accessible.

Not found: a backtest of single-stock VaR or ES on earnings days; the share of the carried variance that is real;
the attribution of flexible models' gains over GARCH to it; a calendar-aware GARCH compared with machine-learning
quantile models. Of six machine-learning-against-GARCH or HAR comparisons on single stocks that were read, one uses
an earnings indicator and five do not mention earnings.

## 8. Fifth pass: both fixes in one GARCH-t, against every model, each given the calendar

paper, not only GARCH-t? Script: `tools/r82_two_fix_garch.py`; output: `results/paper/diagnostics/r82/`. Seven
predictions (T1 to T7) are in the script header, written before the run.

The two-fix GARCH-t is the masked calendar GARCH-t with one more step: the share of today's variance that comes from
the largest shock of the last 63 days is scaled down by a real share that depends on the shock's origin,

The parametric rivals (GARCH-t, GJR-skew-t, Beta-t-EGARCH, SAV-CAViaR, FHS) get the calendar as two multipliers on
their VaR, also chosen on pre-test rows.

### 9.1 What a piece of information is worth to a VaR model (`tools/r83_information_value.py`)

Read with Section 16.4: on the rows it is fitted to this equation runs 0.6 (CRSP) and 1.4 (US large caps) points
high, and the calendar's value grew between the estimation and test periods, so part of the agreement below is two
errors cancelling.

Both errors of GARCH-t are errors of scale on an identifiable set of days. If the standardized return on a day of
type d is `tau_d` times a Student-t with `nu_d` degrees of freedom, the expected pinball loss at level a of a forecast
q is

    EL_d(q) = q (F(q/tau_d) - a) + tau_d f(q/tau_d) (nu_d + (q/tau_d)^2) / (nu_d - 1),

and the value of knowing the day type is

    V(a) = sum_d p_d m_d [EL_d(q) - EL_d(c_d q)] / sum_d p_d m_d EL_d(q),

with `p_d` the share of days of type d, `m_d` their relative volatility and `c_d` the best multiplier. Everything on
the right is estimated before the test period. Nothing in V depends on which model is being helped, which is why
every model gains about the same from the calendar (Section 8.1).

### 9.3 A second clock for the announcement variance (`tools/r85_two_clock.py`)

r78-r82 give every name the same multiplier on its reaction day. Two structural questions.

Multiple or addition. If the announcement added a variance of its own, the squared standardized residual on reaction
days would fall as ordinary volatility rises. It does: by thirds of today's volatility relative to the firm's usual
level it is 20.6, 15.6 and 10.2 on the CRSP panel (test) and 14.4, 12.1 and 8.3 on US large caps. The slope of its log
on the log of relative volatility is -1.21 (s.e. 0.15) and -1.37 (0.08); a pure multiple gives 0 and a pure addition

excess squared return of the firm's last eight announcements; the reaction-day VaR uses `sqrt(s2_t + J2_ij)`. The

## 13. Tenth pass: a ceiling on what any model can gain from a set of inputs (`tools/r90_ceiling.py`)

Write the standardized return of a benchmark as z = s * eps, with eps of the benchmark's shape and s the ratio of the
true scale to the benchmark's. Expanding the expected pinball loss of 9.1 to second order around s = 1, the relative
gain from knowing s is (1/2) kappa E[(log s)^2], with `kappa(a, nu) = q^2 f(q) / EL(q)`. Inputs I reveal only
E[log s | I], so the most any model can gain from them, however flexible, is

    ceiling(I) = (1/2) kappa(a, nu) Var( E[log s | I] ).

E[log s | I] is the regression of log|z| on the inputs: a mean regression, with no quantile model in it. Predictions
U1 to U4 are in the script header.

The exchange rate. kappa is 2.3 (CRSP) and 2.6 (US large caps) at 2.5%, and 2.8 and 3.3 at 1%. A 1% gain in loss needs
a predictable scale error with a standard deviation of about 9% (2.5%) or 8% (1%).

Checked at three sizes (2.5% level):

| Shock share and origin, CRSP | 0.0024 | 0.27% | 0.35% | 0.33% |

| Six past-return inputs, US large caps | 0.0046 | 0.61% | | 0.72% as a multiplier; 1.0% to 1.2% for the flexible quantile models |

| Earnings calendar, US large caps | 0.0168 | 2.2% | 5.3% | 5.5% |

What it shows.

1. Past returns predict very little of the scale of a GARCH residual: 0.8% of the variance of log|z| out of sample
   on the CRSP panel and 0.4% on US large caps. Through kappa that is a gain of about 1%. This is the size of the
   flexible models' margin, and it explains why it is small.
2. The flexible quantile models' margin needs no quantile machinery. A mean regression of log|z| on the same inputs,
   used as a multiplier on the calendar GARCH-t, gains 1.50% on the CRSP panel (DM 7.6), which is inside the range
   of the trees and the network (1.1% to 1.8%), and 0.72% on US large caps (DM 1.9), against 1.0% to 1.2%.
3. The second-order form works for diffuse information and fails for the calendar, where the scale changes by a
   factor of three on a few days; there the exact V of 9.1 is needed.
4. The variance entered is what one regression explains out of sample, so the number is a floor on the true
   ceiling; the realized gains are 0% to 50% above it.

low. On the 2000-2013 panel of Section 14 the equation was 0.5 points high, the direction of the same error. So:

1. The equation converts scale ratios into a loss gain to within about half a point once the tails are fitted on
   the loss side with sigma weights. That is what it can be claimed to do.
2. How well it forecasts is set by how stable the ratios are, and the announcement effect is not stable. The
   bootstrap bands (0.3 to 1.2 points either side) are sampling error only and are narrower than the drift
   between periods (0.9 and 1.9 points).
3. The agreement reported in 9.1 for the two recent panels was in part two errors cancelling and should not be
   described as the equation predicting the calendar's value in advance.
4. Not re-examined here: the portfolio results of 9.1, 14 and 15, which use a different estimate of V (the
   benchmark's own tail and a variance ratio) and matched cell by cell in 720 random books; the partition values of

## 18. One attempt at an equation that returns its own sample: the additive form (`tools/r99_additive_form.py`): fails

Sections 16.4 and 17 found that the equation runs high on the sample it is fitted to, and that one reason is the
fact behind the second clock of 9.3: the announcement adds a variance of its own, which does not scale with the
day's ordinary variance. The attempt: make the informed model additive (the masked filter's variance, plus on a
reaction day the firm's announcement variance from its last eight announcements, with the parameters r85 chose
before the test period), so that the scale ratio to GARCH-t is known for every day, and put that ratio in the nested
form of 16.3 with each day type's tail fitted on the loss side and weighted by the day's weight in the loss sum.
The pass rule was written in the script before the run: the expression within 0.3 points (2.5%) and 0.5 points (1%)
of the gain on the same rows, on the pre-test rows and the test rows of both panels.

| | CRSP pre-test | CRSP test | US pre-test | US test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gain of the additive multipliers over GARCH-t, 2.5% | 8.26 | 9.39 | 5.08 | 7.04 |
| Expression on the same rows | 9.36 | 10.44 | 5.73 | 6.99 |
| Gap, 2.5% | +1.10 | +1.05 | +0.65 | -0.04 |
| Gap, 1% | +0.84 | +0.42 | +1.22 | -0.51 |

It fails: one sample of four is inside the rule at 2.5%. The gaps are no smaller than those of the partition form
with loss-side weighted tails (16.4: -0.31, -0.53, +0.09, -0.20), which remains the version closest to its own
sample. Portfolios do not pass either: for single names the realized gain is 0.81 and 1.02 of V on the CRSP panel
and 0.99 and 1.44 on US large caps (pre-test days, test days).

A reading that was not tested: the expression credits the gain of knowing each day's scale exactly, and the firm's
announcement variance from eight announcements is a noisy estimate of it.

Two things the attempt shows that do not depend on the expression. The additive multipliers gain 9.4% and 7.0% over
GARCH-t on the test rows, against 7.7% and 5.7% for one constant multiplier (16.3) and 9.3% to 10.7% and 7.0% to
7.7% for the flexible quantile models with the calendar; and fitted before the test period the expression is 0.6
and 0.3 points below the test rows' gain at 2.5%, closer than the constant-multiplier versions, because the firm's
own history follows the drift in the announcement effect. With the overshoot above, that closeness cannot be
claimed as accuracy.

Where the equation stands after Sections 16 to 18. It is an accounting of what a known scale difference costs a
VaR model. Its best version (day types, tails on the loss side, sigma weights) is within about half a point of its
own sample at 2.5% on two panels. It orders information sets correctly and gives the decay with portfolio size. It
does not forecast the level of the calendar's value across periods, and no version tried returns its own sample
within 0.3 points everywhere.
